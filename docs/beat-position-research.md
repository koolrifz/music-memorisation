# Is "keep the beat on the right pad" too much for grades 5 and 6?

**Asked by Rob, 2026-10-08**, after four classes of seven clarinets (grades 5
and 6) played Beat Smash on an iPad:

> *"Having the different buttons, one, two, three, and four, and reading
> something up above and following the one, two, three, four... I just wonder
> if we should do a quick investigation to make sure that I'm not making them
> do something too complicated."* And: *"moving left to right, I think that's
> the biggest hurdle that I saw... let's make sure I'm not asking too much...
> I don't want to make anyone disinterested in music."*

A short reading of the literature, not a systematic review. Where a claim
rests on a summary rather than the paper itself, it says so.

## What the four pads ask for

Three things at once:

1. **Keeping a steady beat** (synchronising taps to the band).
2. **Knowing where you are in the bar**, and showing it: beat 3 on pad 3, even
   through rests, when nothing is played and the place has to be kept inside.
3. **Playing a rhythm on top of it**, read from the squares or the notation:
   the notes, not every beat.

One pad asks for 1 and 3. The four pads add 2 and make it visible. That is
exactly the "rubbing the belly and patting the head" Rob described: the beat
and the rhythm, held together.

## What the research says

- **The steady beat is still maturing at 10–12.** Tapping gets steadier with
  age through childhood; children tap more variably than adults, and the
  coordination of two movements to a beat keeps developing. A Southampton
  study of walking-and-clapping found children's coupling looked like adults'
  by 8 and as consistent by 10, but the harder gallop-and-clap was **not
  adult-like by 10**. ([Southampton][soton]; [Frontiers 2014 review][front])
- **Slower is easier, and the tapping-rhythm link is strongest at slow
  tempi.** In 119 children aged 7–12, tapping consistency predicted rhythm
  discrimination more strongly at 60 and 100 bpm than at 120 and 180. And in
  9–12-year-olds at risk of coordination difficulties, step-clap coordination
  fell apart at fast tempi but matched their peers at slow ones.
  ([Kim, Lee & Lee 2023][kim]; [Carrillo, Dotov & Trainor 2025][trainor])
- **By 10–12, working memory takes over.** In the same 7–12 study, beat
  tapping predicted rhythm skill at 7–9, but at 10–12 only auditory working
  memory did. Reading notation while holding your place in the bar is a
  working-memory load, so for this age group the reading and the place-keeping
  compete for the same resource. ([Kim, Lee & Lee 2023][kim])
- **Beat before rhythm, by design.** Phyllis Weikart's sequential approach
  held back moving to rhythm patterns until a child could keep a steady beat.
  The warm-up is that first stage; the reading steps are the second.
  ([Weikart, as summarised in a kindergarten study][weikart])
- **Left to right is not the hard part.** Readers of left-to-right languages
  already lay time out left to right; the mental timeline emerges around 5 and
  sets as children learn to read. Pads 1 2 3 4 from left to right go with
  that, not against it. (A summary of [Tillman et al. and others][time]; the
  child studies themselves were not read.)
- **Pointing along the beats is early-primary work.** Kodály-style classrooms
  have children tap a strip of four hearts left to right, beat by beat, in the
  first years of school, and put rhythms on top later.
  ([Berks Music Trust][berks]; [The Flute Examiner][flute];
  [LAUSD K–1 module][lausd])

## So: too much?

**No, but it is a real skill and needs a ladder, which the research supports
building rather than avoiding.** Left to right is natural to them. What is
hard is keeping their place through the rests while reading, at a tempo, and
that is the skill Rob wants: knowing where beat 1 is without stopping to work
it out. Three levers the research points at:

1. **Beat first, secured, then the rhythm on top** (Weikart). The warm-up's
   guide now does this: the count-in, the tick, the green walk until the beat
   is established, and the walk back whenever it is lost.
2. **Slow it down for the first reading steps.** The evidence is clearest on
   tempo. The first song plays at 100 bpm; 80–90 for the one-bar and two-bar
   steps would ease the load without changing the task. (Night Owl and Skate
   Park are already 80.)
3. **Practise the place-keeping on its own, as play**, before or beside the
   reading: games of this level with no notation at all (call and response
   with Tango; marching the beat while clapping quavers). See
   `ideas/rhythm-rudiments.md`.

One proposal stays Rob's call: carrying a faint version of the green walk into
the first star's picture go, so the first reading take has the pad shown as
well as the square. It would be scaffolding that comes down by the third star,
like the squares.
